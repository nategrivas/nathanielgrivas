What it’s like

North of Montana Avenue is the quiet, polished side: wide streets, big trees and Montana's boutiques and coffee. Downtown has the Third Street Promenade, the Pier and the Metro E Line terminal at 4th and Colorado. Main Street and Ocean Park to the south feel looser and closer to Venice.

Santa Monica is its own city, about eight square miles of bluffs and flats between Pacific Palisades, Brentwood and Venice. It runs its own transfer tax, rent control and school district, so the rules change the second you cross the city line.

## The pockets of Santa Monica

[Every LA pocket on one map →](https://www.nathanielgrivas.com/neighborhoods/map)

In Los Angeles a single street can change the price of a house. Santa Monica is really 9 smaller neighborhoods, and these are the lines between them, drawn street by street. Dashed outlines are the pockets next door.

### North of Montana

Montana Avenue north to the city line at the canyon rim, Ocean Avenue to 26th Street

Santa Monica's estate grid: wide, tree-lined numbered streets of large single-family homes, with Montana Avenue's shops at the bottom and San Vicente's coral-tree median running through. It is the only Santa Monica neighborhood with its own private patrol.

### Gold Coast

The beachfront strip along Palisades Beach Road, below the Palisades Park bluffs

A single row of houses on the sand north of the pier, many dating to the studio era of the 1920s and 30s. Lots back straight onto the beach, with the Annenberg Community Beach House in the middle.

### Wilshire Montana

Wilshire Boulevard to Montana Avenue, Ocean Avenue to 26th Street

Condos and small apartment buildings near the ocean that give way to single-family streets as you head east toward 26th. Walkable to Montana Avenue, Wilshire and Downtown.

### Northeast Santa Monica

Wilshire Boulevard to Montana Avenue east of 26th Street, to the Los Angeles line

A small, quiet pocket of single-family homes and low-rise condos on the Brentwood border, with Santa Monica schools and city services.

### Downtown Santa Monica

Ocean Avenue to Lincoln Boulevard, Wilshire Boulevard to Pico Boulevard

The Third Street Promenade, the pier, the Expo line terminus and the Civic Center. Mostly condos, lofts and rentals, a few blocks from the sand.

### Mid-City

Lincoln Boulevard to the city line, Wilshire Boulevard to Olympic Boulevard

Santa Monica's medical and commercial middle, with both of the city's hospitals and a mix of condos, apartments and single-family streets.

### Pico Neighborhood

Olympic Boulevard to Pico Boulevard, Lincoln Boulevard to Centinela Avenue

Virginia Avenue Park and the Bergamot arts district anchor this mixed pocket of bungalows, duplexes and apartments between the freeway and Pico.

### Ocean Park

Pico Boulevard to the Venice line, the beach to Lincoln Boulevard

Main Street's shops, Ocean Park Boulevard and walk-street cottages near the sand. Small lots, Craftsman bungalows and modern rebuilds sit side by side.

### Sunset Park

Pico Boulevard to the city line, Lincoln Boulevard east to the airport

Santa Monica's family pocket: blocks of 1940s and 50s single-family homes around Clover Park and the airport, with Ocean Park Boulevard's cafes and Santa Monica schools.

The story of Santa Monica

## The city on the sea that almost became LA's port

Santa Monica was laid out in 1875 by a silver-rich senator who wanted a seaport. It lost the harbor fight to San Pedro and became a beach town instead, and along the way it built California's oldest pleasure pier, launched the first flight around the world and became the end of the most famous highway in America.

ICity on the sea

### A port that wasn't

**1875** the first land sale, July 10

Santa Monica began as a business plan. Col. Robert Baker bought most of the old Sepulveda rancho in 1872, and Senator John P. Jones, rich from Nevada silver, bought in two years later. They laid out a town in 1875, set aside the bluff-top park now called Palisades Park, and planned a seaport that would make it the gateway to Los Angeles.

For a while it looked possible. Southern Pacific built the Long Wharf near the mouth of Santa Monica Canyon in the 1890s and ran trains out over the water. But the fight over where the federal government would build LA's harbor went to San Pedro in 1897, and Santa Monica turned to what it does best: sun, sand and visitors.

IIThe pier

### A sewage pipe that became a landmark

**1909** California's oldest pleasure pier

The pier that defines the city was built in 1909 to carry a sewage line out to sea, and anglers took it over almost immediately. In 1916 amusement builder Charles Looff, who had built Coney Island's first carousel, added a pleasure pier beside it with rides and the Hippodrome, an exotic building made to hold a carousel. The Hippodrome still turns one today.

IIIWings

### The first flight around the world

**200,000** people at Clover Field to welcome the fliers home

In 1922 Donald Douglas set up an aircraft factory at Clover Field, today's Santa Monica Airport. Two years later four of his Douglas World Cruisers took off to try to circle the globe. Two made it, covering more than 27,000 miles in 175 days, and came home on September 23, 1924 to a crowd of about 200,000. During the Second World War the Douglas plant employed as many as 44,000 people, and its workers filled the bungalows and apartments that still line the city's side streets.
